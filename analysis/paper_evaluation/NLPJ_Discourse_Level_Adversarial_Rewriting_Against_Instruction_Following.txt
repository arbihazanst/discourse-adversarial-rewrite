ABSTRACT
Instruction-followinglargelanguagemodels(LLMs)areincreasinglyusedaszero-shottextclassifiers,
including in email spam detection. However, adversarial robustness evaluation for such systems is
still largely shaped by legacy attack paradigms developed for conventional neural classifiers rather
than prompt-conditioned LLMs. This paper investigates whether classical GAN-based adversarial
rewriting remains effective in the LLM era and compares it with a two-step adaptive LLM-based
rewriting attacker under a realistic black-box, label-only threat model. We evaluate nine victim
modelsspanningproprietaryAPI-basedsystemsandopen-weightlocallydeployedmodels:GPT-5.2,
DeepSeek-V3.1,Llama-4-Maverick:17B,Qwen-2.5:32B,Gemma-3:27B,GPT-OSS:20B,DeepSeek-
R1:1.5B, Gemma-3:1B, and Qwen-2.5:0.5B. Experiments are conducted on a balanced dataset of
3,000 emails (1,500 spam and 1,500 non-spam). Adversarial rewriting is applied only to the true-
positive spam pool of each model, yielding attack sets ranging from 332 to 1,479 samples. Results
show that the classical GAN-based attack has limited effectiveness against most larger instruction-
following LLM classifiers, with attack success rates below 2% for five models and near-zero
vulnerability for Gemma-3:27B and Llama-4-Maverick:17B. In contrast, the adaptive LLM-based
attacker consistently achieves higher evasion rates, reaching 5.22%–12.93% on stronger models and
substantially higher rates on smaller models. To examine whether attack success is associated with
semanticdegradation,wefurtherevaluateadversarialrewritesusingSBERT,BLEU,ROUGE-L,and
Jaccard similarity. The results show that SBERT similarity remains relatively high across models,
while lexical overlap decreases more noticeably, especially after the second rewrite step. Outcome-
basedanalysisfurthershowsthatsuccessfuladversarialrewritestypicallyexhibitnegativeconditional
similaritydifferencesforlexicalmetrics,indicatingloweroverlapwiththeoriginalemailsthanfailed
attacks,whileSBERTdifferencesremainrelativelysmall.Thispatternsuggeststhateffectiveevasion
is achieved primarily through discourse-level and surface-form reformulation rather than semantic
deletion. This indicates that successful attacks are primarily achieved through discourse-level and
surface-form transformations rather than semantic deletion. Overall, the findings suggest that legacy
GAN-based attacks underestimate the practical evasion risk faced by instruction-following LLM
classifiers, whereas adaptive LLM-based rewriting provides a more realistic robustness benchmark
for LLM-based text classification.

2.3. Sentence-Level Adversarial Attacks
Beyond token-level substitutions, generation-based at-
tacks aim to produce fully rewritten adversarial examples
while preserving semantic intent. Sequence generative ad-
versarial networks (SeqGAN) Yu, Zhang, Wang and Yu
(2017) introduced reinforcement-learning-based sequence
generation for discrete tokens. Subsequent work explored
generative approaches for adversarial rewriting Zhao, Dua
and Singh (2017).
Zhaoetal.Zhaoetal.(2017)proposedgeneratingnatural
and legible adversarial examples through generative mod-

detection while preserving meaning. GAN-based rewriting
methods were widely adopted for adversarial evaluation in
text classification tasks Iyyer, Wieting, Gimpel and Zettle-
moyer (2018); Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin (2018).
Nevertheless, sentence-level generation methods often
struggle with long documents due to coherence constraints
and exposure bias. As noted in the SEA framework Ribeiro
et al. (2018), paraphrase models tend to preserve sentence
structure and may fail to generate certain adversarial trans-
formations. These limitations become more pronounced in
long-form emails, where discourse structure and pragmatic
cues influence classification.
Previous studies highlight that adversarial attacks in
natural language processing must carefully preserve seman-
tic coherence while still perturbing the model’s decision
boundary, which makes the generation of effective textual
adversarial examples significantly more challenging than in
continuousdomainssuchascomputervisionQiu,Liu,Zhou

3. Threat Model
We formalize a realistic adversarial setting tailored to
API-deployed, prompt-based large language model (LLM)
spam classifiers. Unlike classical adversarial NLP scenarios
thatassumewhite-boxaccess,gradientavailability,orprob-
ability scores, our threat model reflects the operational con-
straints of modern LLM-based filtering systems deployed
through cloud APIs.
The primary objective of this threat modeling exercise
is to ensure that robustness evaluation aligns with realis-
tic adversarial capabilities rather than idealized laboratory
assumptions. In practical spam ecosystems, attackers do
not have access to model weights or internal confidence
scores. Instead, they observe binary accept/reject behavior
and iteratively refine content accordingly. Our formulation
is intentionally grounded in this operational reality.

3.5. Adversary Knowledge and Capabilities
Wemodelapartiallyinformedadversaryconsistentwith
real-world spammers. The attacker is aware that the task is
spam classification and understands the binary output label
space (SPAM vs. NOT_SPAM). However, the adversary is
model-agnostic and lacks knowledge of whether the victim

is restricted to rewriting the email text only; system-level

jailbreak-style exploitation.
3.6. Query Budget Constraints
Query constraints are essential for realistic benchmark-
ing. The classical GAN-based rewriting attack is applied
using its standard generative mechanism over the full true-
positivespampool.Incontrast,theadaptiveLLMattackeris
restricted to at most two rewrite attempts: an initial rewrite
followed by a single refinement if necessary. No further
iterations are permitted. This bounded protocol limits the
number of rewriting attempts and enables controlled com-
parison between attack strategies. The two-step design re-
flects a realistic black-box interaction in which the attacker
receives only the classifier label as feedback and performs a
limited refinement of the rewritten email.

4.8. Semantic Preservation Evaluation Protocol
Adversarial rewriting attacks aim to alter classifier pre-
dictionswhilepreservingtheunderlyingsemanticcontentof
the original message. To ensure that attack success reflects
decision-boundary manipulation rather than trivial content
deletionorsemanticdrift,weconductasystematicsemantic
preservation evaluation between each original spam email
and its corresponding adversarial rewrites.
4.8.1. Motivation
Whilerewriting-basedattacksconstrainmodificationsto
stylistic framing, tone, and discourse structure, it remains
necessary to verify empirically that successful label flips
are not primarily caused by degradation of core commu-
nicative intent. In particular, an adversarial example should
retainnamedentities,numericalvalues,offers,requests,and
overall semantic objective, even if persuasive cues such
as urgency or capitalization are softened. Without explicit
semantic validation, attack success could be misinterpreted
as content removal rather than robustness failure.
4.8.2. Similarity Metrics
We evaluate semantic preservation using a combination
of embedding-based and lexical similarity metrics.
Embedding-Based Semantic Similarity.We compute
cosine similarity between sentence embeddings of the orig-
inal email and each rewritten version using a pretrained
Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model. Let𝑥denote the original
email and𝑥′ its adversarial rewrite. Let𝐞(𝑥)and𝐞(𝑥 ′)be
their normalized embedding vectors. Semantic similarity is
defined as:
SimSBERT(𝑥, 𝑥′)=cos (𝐞(𝑥),𝐞(𝑥′)).(9)
SBERT is treated as the primary semantic preservation
metricduetoitsabilitytocapturecontextualandparaphras-
tic similarity beyond surface-level token overlap.

report lexical overlap metrics, including BLEU, ROUGE-
L (F1), and Jaccard similarity. BLEU evaluates n-gram
precisionoverlap,ROUGE-Lcaptureslongestcommonsub-
sequence alignment, and Jaccard similarity measures token
set overlap:
Jaccard(𝑥, 𝑥′)= |𝑇(𝑥)∩𝑇(𝑥 ′)|

where𝑇(⋅)denotesthesetofuniquetokensafternormal-

Embedding-based metrics primarily assess semantic
preservation, whereas lexical metrics quantify surface vari-

Similarity is computed for both the first rewrite and the

4.8.4. Outcome-Based Separation
To examine whether attack success is associated with
semantic degradation, we separate adversarial samples into
two groups:
•Successful flips:={𝑥 ′∣(𝑥 ′)=NOT_SPAM},
•Failed attacks:={𝑥 ′∣(𝑥 ′)=SPAM}.

This separation enables analysis of whether classifica-

6. Discussion
Thestatisticalanalysisfurtherconfirmsthatthesediffer-

pool. Furthermore, two-proportion significance tests indi-

thattheimprovedevasioncapabilityofinstruction-following

The content retention analysis further indicates that
classical GAN-based attacks often succeed by generating
severely truncated or content-degraded messages, whereas
LLM-based adversarial rewriting preserves both message
length and key semantic elements, suggesting that modern
attacks operate primarily at the discourse level rather than
through simple lexical perturbations.
The extremely low extreme-collapse rate observed for
LLM-generatedadversarialsamplesfurthersupportsthehy-

than through semantic degradation.
6.1. Why Legacy GAN-Based Attacks
Underestimate LLM-Era Vulnerability
ThecomparisonbetweenGAN-basedandadaptiveLLM-
based attacks shows a consistent gap in adversarial effec-
tiveness across most victim models. Classical GAN-based
rewriting achieves very low attack success rates for most
larger instruction-following LLMs, whereas adaptive LLM-
based rewriting yields substantially higher evasion rates.
This contrast suggests that legacy sentence-level generation
attacks do not sufficiently target the discourse-level and
pragmatic cues that modern prompt-based classifiers rely
on. In other words, when the decision boundary depends
not only on lexical triggers but also on tone, framing, and
communicative style, an instruction-following adversarial
rewriter is better aligned with the victim model’s reasoning
process than a legacy GAN-based generator.

6.2. Robustness is Strongly Scale-Dependent
Aclearrobustnessgradientemergesacrossmodelscales.
Larger models such as GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3.1, Qwen-
2.5:32B, and Llama-4-Maverick:17B remain relatively re-
sistant to both attack types, although they still exhibit non-
trivial vulnerability under adaptive LLM-based rewriting.
In contrast, smaller models such as DeepSeek-R1:1.5B,
Gemma-3:1B,andQwen-2.5:0.5Bshowsubstantiallyhigher
attack success rates, especially under the two-step adaptive
attack. This pattern suggests that scale is associated not
onlywithbetterbaselineclassificationperformance,butalso
with stronger robustness to paraphrastic and discourse-level
adversarialmanipulation.Thefindingisparticularlyrelevant
for practical deployment, where lightweight models may
be attractive for efficiency reasons but considerably weaker
under adversarial stress.
6.3. Iterative Rewriting Matters
The step-wise analysis further shows that adversarial
effectiveness is not solely determined by a single rewrite
attempt. The first rewriting step accounts for the majority
of successful evasions in most models, but the second step
consistentlyprovidesadditionalgains,especiallyforsmaller
victims. This indicates that even minimal feedback in a
black-box, label-only setting can be exploited effectively.
From a benchmarking perspective, this result is important
becauseasingle-stepattackwouldunderestimatethevulner-
ability of some models, particularly those whose decision
boundaries can be crossed only after iterative reduction of
spam-associated cues.
6.4. Semantic Preservation Versus Surface
Transformation
The similarity analysis provides an important interpre-
tive layer for the attack results. SBERT similarity remains
relatively high across successful and unsuccessful adversar-
ialsamples,indicatingthatthesemanticcontentoftheorig-
inal spam message is largely preserved. In contrast, BLEU,
ROUGE-L, and Jaccard show more substantial reductions,
particularly in Step 2. This divergence suggests that the
adaptive attacker succeeds primarily by altering lexical and
structural realization rather than by deleting or replacing
the underlying communicative intent. The conditional sim-
ilarity results strengthen this interpretation. Across most
models,thelexicalmetricsinTables10–12exhibitnegative
Δvalues, indicating that successful attacks usually produce
lower lexical overlap than failed attacks, particularly during
the second rewrite step. In contrast, SBERT differences in
Table9remainrelativelysmall,suggestingthatthesemantic
intentoftheoriginalspammessageislargelypreservedeven
when lexical and structural realization changes enough to
flip the classifier decision. In practical terms, the classifier
is fooled not because the message ceases to be spam in
meaning,butbecauseitsdiscourse-levelrealizationbecomes
less overtly spam-like.
6.5. Implications for Robustness Benchmarking in
NLP
These findings have broader implications beyond spam
detection.First,robustnessevaluationforinstruction-following
LLM classifiers should not rely exclusively on legacy ad-
versarial attacks inherited from pre-LLM text classification
settings. Second, semantic preservation analysis should be
treated as an essential component of adversarial evaluation,
particularly for rewriting-based attacks. Third, adaptive
multi-step rewriting under black-box feedback provides a
realistic and scalable benchmark for modern LLM-based
classifiers. Taken together, the results support a shift from
token-level or sentence-template adversarial testing toward
discourse-aware, LLM-native robustness evaluation proto-
cols.
7. Conclusion
This paper presented a comparative robustness evalua-
tion of instruction-following LLM classifiers under two ad-
versarial rewriting paradigms: classical GAN-based rewrit-
ing and a two-step adaptive LLM-based attacker. Using
nine victim models and a balanced spam detection dataset,
we showed that legacy GAN-based attacks have limited
effectiveness against most larger modern LLM classifiers,
whereasadaptiveLLM-basedrewritingconsistentlyachieves
higher evasion rates and therefore reveals a substantially
stronger practical threat.
The results also show that robustness varies markedly
across model scales. Larger models remain comparatively
resistant, while smaller open-weight models exhibit much
higher vulnerability, particularly under iterative rewriting.
The step-wise analysis further demonstrates that a second
rewriteattemptcanprovidemeaningfuladditionalgains,in-
dicating that even minimal black-box feedback can improve
adversarial effectiveness.
Importantly, the semantic preservation analysis shows
that successful attacks generally maintain relatively high
embedding-based similarity while reducing lexical over-
lap. Outcome-based conditional similarity analysis further
shows that successful attacks typically exhibit only small
SBERT differences but more consistently negative lexical
differences, indicating that effective evasion is associated
more with surface-form reformulation than with semantic
drift. This reinforces the interpretation that modern LLM-
based adversarial rewriting operates primarily at the dis-
course and stylistic levels. This suggests that adversarial
success is driven primarily by discourse-level and surface-
form transformation rather than semantic deletion. Taken
together, these findings indicate that legacy adversarial at-
tacks can underestimate the vulnerability of prompt-based
LLM classifiers, and that adaptive LLM-based rewriting
provides a more realistic robustness benchmark for modern
NLP classification systems.
