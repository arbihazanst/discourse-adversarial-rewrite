ABSTRACTInstruction-following large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as zero-shot
text classifiers, including in email spam detection. However, adversarial robustness evaluation for such
systems is still largely shaped by legacy attack paradigms developed for conventional neural classifiers
rather than prompt-conditioned LLMs. This paper investigates whether classical GAN-based adversarial
rewriting remains effective in the LLM era and compares it with a two-step adaptive LLM-based rewriting
attacker under a realistic black-box, label-only threat model. We evaluate nine victim models spanning
proprietary API-based systems and open-weight locally deployed models: GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3.1, Llama-
4-Maverick:17B, Qwen-2.5:32B, Gemma-3:27B, GPT-OSS:20B, DeepSeek-R1:1.5B, Gemma-3:1B, and
Qwen-2.5:0.5B. Experiments are conducted on a balanced dataset of 3,000 emails (1,500 spam and 1,500
non-spam). Adversarial rewriting is applied only to the true-positive spam pool of each model, yielding
attack sets ranging from 332 to 1,479 samples. Results show that the classical GAN-based attack has
limited effectiveness against most larger instruction-following LLM classifiers, with attack success rates
below 2% for five models and near-zero vulnerability for Gemma-3:27B and Llama-4-Maverick:17B. In
contrast, the adaptive LLM-based attacker consistently achieves higher evasion rates, reaching 5.22%–
12.93% on stronger models and substantially higher rates on smaller models. To examine whether attack
success is associated with semantic degradation, we further evaluate adversarial rewrites using SBERT,
BLEU, ROUGE-L, and Jaccard similarity. The results show that SBERT similarity remains relatively
high across models, while lexical overlap decreases more noticeably, especially after the second rewrite
step. Outcome-based analysis further shows that successful adversarial rewrites typically exhibit negative
conditional similarity differences for lexical metrics, indicating lower overlap with the original emails than
failed attacks, while SBERT differences remain relatively small. This pattern suggests that effective evasion
is achieved primarily through discourse-level and surface-form reformulation rather than semantic deletion.
This indicates that successful attacks are primarily achieved through discourse-level and surface-form
transformations rather than semantic deletion. Overall, the findings suggest that legacy GAN-based attacks
underestimate the practical evasion risk faced by instruction-following LLM classifiers, whereas adaptive
LLM-based rewriting provides a more realistic robustness benchmark for LLM-based text classification.

I. INTRODUCTION
Email spam remains a persistent and evolving threat, en-
abling fraud, phishing, and malware distribution at scale.
While classical machine learning pipelines for spam detection
have matured over decades, recent system trends increasingly
incorporate Large Language Models (LLMs) aszero-shot
orfew-shotclassifiers via prompting, motivated by rapid
deployment, improved semantic generalization, and reduced
feature engineering overhead. Y et, the adversarial robustness
of LLM-based spam classifiers remains under-explored under
realistic threat models.
Adversarial NLP research has established that text classi-
fiers can be manipulated through carefully crafted perturba-
tions and paraphrases [1]–[4]. Toolkits such as TextAttack
and OpenAttack enable standardized evaluation of multiple
attack families [5], [6].
Adversarial machine learning has also been extensively
studied in broader cybersecurity contexts, particularly in in-
trusion detection systems (IDS) deployed in industrial control
systems (ICS). In such environments, attackers may manip-
ulate sensor data or system inputs to evade detection by
machine-learning-based IDS models. Recent work has ex-
plored adversarial attack detection mechanisms using neural
architectures such as LSTM-based intrusion detection models
and black-box defense strategies, demonstrating that adver-
sarial manipulation can significantly affect the reliability of
automated security monitoring systems [7]. These studies
highlight the practical importance of evaluating adversarial
robustness in security-critical machine learning deployments.
However, many classical attacks were developed forstatic
neural classifiers and rely on perturbation mechanisms (e.g.,
token substitution, gradient-guided triggers, or template-
based paraphrasing) that do not directly align with the behav-
ior of instruction-following LLMs deployed through prompt-
ing interfaces or APIs. Unlike traditional adversarial pertur-
bations that operate primarily at the token or sentence level,
rewriting-based attacks can manipulate discourse structure
and stylistic cues while preserving semantic intent, making
them particularly relevant for evaluating the robustness of
instruction-following LLM classifiers.
Consequently, robustness evaluation for modern LLM clas-
sifiers requires adversarial strategies that operate at the dis-
course level rather than relying solely on local token-level
perturbations. Despite the success of Generative Adversarial
Network (GAN)-based methods for data augmentation in cy-
bersecurity tasks, their effectiveness as adversarial rewriting
mechanisms against modern LLM-based classifiers remains
largely unexplored.
This paper is motivated by a practical gap:Does a classical
sentence-level GAN-based rewriting attack remain effective
against modern LLM spam classifiers?If not,what is a
minimal, low-compute, but more realistic attacker model for
LLM-era spam filtering?In particular, attackers in real-world
spam operations frequently rephrase and reframe messages
to avoid heuristic cues and moderation triggers. LLMs have
made such rewriting scalable and fluent; therefore, anLLM-
based adversarial rewriteris a plausible attacker for practical
security evaluation.
This study makes several key contributions to the evalua-
tion of adversarial robustness in LLM-based spam detection.
First, we establish a nine-model benchmark for zero-shot
LLM spam classification, evaluating both proprietary and
open-source large language models under a unified classi-
fication prompt on a balanced dataset of 3,000 emails. This
provides a consistent and comparable foundation for robust-
ness analysis across heterogeneous deployments. Second, we
conduct a systematic comparison between a classical GAN-
based rewriting attack and an adaptive LLM-based adver-
sarial attacker. Specifically, we measure and contrast attack
success rates when applying a sentence-level GAN rewriting
method over each model’s true-positive spam pool and when
deploying a two-step adaptive LLM attacker, using Gemma-
3:27B as the adversarial generator. This comparison enables
us to examine whether legacy adversarial techniques remain
effective in the LLM era and whether LLM-native attackers
represent a stronger and more realistic threat model. Third,
we analyze the robustness of instruction-following LLM clas-
sifiers under iterative adversarial rewriting by decomposing
the attack process into two sequential steps. This step-wise
analysis provides insight into how adaptive rewriting progres-
sively alters surface-level linguistic cues while preserving the
semantic content of the original email.
Finally, we present a transparent and reproducible eval-
uation protocol, described step by step, while responsibly
redacting exact attacker prompt strings to prevent direct mis-
use for real-world spam evasion. This approach balances
reproducibility for authorized research with ethical consid-
erations in security-sensitive domains.
II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
The adversarial robustness of text classification systems has
evolved through multiple paradigm shifts, from classical
feature-based spam filtering to transformer-based contextual
models and, most recently, prompt-driven large language
model (LLM) classifiers. In this section, we review the his-
torical progression of spam detection, the development of
adversarial NLP methods, generation-based attacks such as
GAN rewriting, and emerging LLM-native security threats.
We situate our work at the intersection of adversarial NLP
and LLM-based security evaluation.
A. SPAM DETECTION: FROM STATISTICAL MODELS TO
LLM-BASED CLASSIFICATION
Email spam detection has been a foundational problem in
applied machine learning for over two decades. Early ap-
proaches relied on rule-based filtering and statistical classi-
fiers such as Naïve Bayes, logistic regression, and support
vector machines [8], [9]. These systems typically depended
on handcrafted lexical features, token frequency statistics,
and manually curated heuristics.
More recent phishing-detection research demonstrates that
conventional supervised learning pipelines can remain highly

effective when feature engineering, class balancing, and en-
semble learning are carefully integrated. Ramaiah et al. [10]
combined URL-, JavaScript-, and anomaly-based indicators
with SMOTE-based class balancing and DT-RFECV fea-
ture selection, followed by an ensemble stacking classifier,
achieving high detection accuracy across two benchmark
datasets. Although this approach targets phishing websites
rather than prompt-conditioned email classification, it illus-

ventional detection pipelines and provides a useful contrast to
the zero-shot LLM-based classification setting considered in

The introduction of distributed word representations and
deep learning architectures improved semantic modeling ca-
pabilities [11]. Transformer architectures [12] further ad-
vanced contextual representation learning, enabling pre-
trained language models such as BERT [13] and RoBERTa
to significantly improve text classification accuracy across
domains. Spam detection systems increasingly adopted fine-
tuned transformer encoders for semantic robustness [14],
[15].
More recently, large-scale generative language models
have been used as zero-shot or few-shot classifiers through
prompting [16]. In the cybersecurity domain, Nasution et
al. [17] benchmarked 21 open-source LLMs for phishing-link
detection under zero-shot, role-playing, chain-of-thought,
and few-shot prompting, showing that large open-source
models can exceed 90% F1 without task-specific fine-tuning
while also demonstrating that prompting strategy can sub-
stantially affect both classification performance and com-
putational cost. Instead of task-specific fine-tuning, LLM-
based classification leverages instruction-following capabil-
ities. This shift reduces feature engineering and retraining
overhead while improving generalization across heteroge-
neous spam formats. However, robustness evaluation method-
ologies for LLM-based classifiers remain underdeveloped
compared to classical neural classifiers.
As noted in recent security-focused evaluations [18], [19],
LLM-integrated systems introduce new threat surfaces due to
their instruction-following nature and sensitivity to discourse
framing. Y et, systematic adversarial benchmarking for LLM-
based spam detection remains sparse.
While classification architectures have evolved from statis-
tical models to instruction-following LLMs, robustness eval-
uation methodologies have not evolved at the same pace. To
understand this gap, we review the progression of adversarial
attack strategies in Natural Language Processing (NLP).
B. CHARACTER / WORD-LEVEL ADVERSARIAL ATTACKS
To understand how robustness has traditionally been eval-
uated, we first review adversarial attack methods in NLP .
Adversarial machine learning was initially studied in com-
puter vision [1], where small perturbations could drastically
change classifier outputs. In NLP , the discrete nature of text
complicates gradient-based perturbations, leading to search-
based and substitution-based attacks.
Early textual adversarial methods focused on character-
level perturbations [20] and synonym substitutions [2].
TextFooler [3] demonstrated that semantically constrained
word replacements could reliably flip neural classifier pre-
dictions. Contextual attacks such as BERT-Attack [4] lever-
aged masked language models to generate fluent adversarial
tokens.
Universal adversarial triggers [21] further demonstrated
the vulnerability of NLP systems to systematic token manipu-
lations. Frameworks such as TextAttack [5] and OpenAttack
[6] standardized benchmarking across multiple attack fami-
lies.
However, most classical attacks were designed for static,
fine-tuned classifiers with accessible logits or gradients.
LLM-based spam classifiers, especially API-deployed mod-
els, operate under black-box, label-only conditions and rely
heavily on discourse-level semantics rather than token-level
features. Consequently, legacy attacks may not reflect realis-
tic threat models in modern LLM deployments.
Although these attacks demonstrated vulnerability in static
neural classifiers, their relevance to modern instruction-
following LLMs remains unclear. This motivates the exam-
ination of higher-level rewriting strategies.
C. SENTENCE-LEVEL ADVERSARIAL ATTACKS
Beyond token-level substitutions, generation-based attacks
aim to produce fully rewritten adversarial examples while
preserving semantic intent. Sequence generative adversarial
networks (SeqGAN) [22] introduced reinforcement-learning-
based sequence generation for discrete tokens. Subsequent
work explored generative approaches for adversarial rewrit-
ing [23].
Zhao et al. [23] proposed generating natural and legible ad-
versarial examples through generative modeling, highlighting
that fluent adversarial texts can evade detection while pre-
serving meaning. GAN-based rewriting methods were widely
adopted for adversarial evaluation in text classification tasks
[24], [25].
Nevertheless, sentence-level generation methods often
struggle with long documents due to coherence constraints
and exposure bias. As noted in the SEA framework [25], para-
phrase models tend to preserve sentence structure and may
fail to generate certain adversarial transformations. These
limitations become more pronounced in long-form emails,
where discourse structure and pragmatic cues influence clas-
sification.
Previous studies highlight that adversarial attacks in natural
language processing must carefully preserve semantic coher-
ence while still perturbing the model’s decision boundary,
which makes the generation of effective textual adversarial
examples significantly more challenging than in continuous
domains such as computer vision [26]. Because textual data
are discrete and semantically structured, successful attacks
must operate within linguistic constraints while still influ-
encing the internal representation used by the target model.
As a result, adversarial example generation methods are of-

D. LLM-BASED AND ADAPTIVE ADVERSARIAL ATTACKS
As classification systems evolve toward instruction-following
LLMs, adversarial strategies must also adapt to these new
reasoning dynamics. LLMs introduce new operational char-
acteristics compared to traditional classifiers. Rather than
relying solely on latent embeddings and classification heads,
instruction-following LLMs integrate semantic reasoning,
contextual framing, and discourse interpretation.
Prompt injection attacks [18], [19] demonstrate that LLM
outputs can be manipulated through carefully structured input
content. While spam detection is a classification problem
rather than a generative application, adversarial rewriting can
be interpreted as manipulating the input discourse to alter the
model’s classification reasoning.
Furthermore, adaptive black-box attacks using LLMs as
adversarial generators have begun to emerge [27], [28].
These approaches leverage the generative fluency of LLMs to
produce high-quality adversarial inputs under query-limited
settings. Compared to classical token substitution, LLM-
based rewriting enables discourse-level reframing that better
matches how modern classifiers interpret intent and tone.
Recent studies highlight that adversarial attacks against
LLMs exploit several architectural characteristics unique to
these models. In particular, instruction-following models of-
ten struggle to clearly distinguish between instructions and
contextual data within input sequences. This ambiguity al-
lows malicious inputs to embed adversarial directives within
seemingly benign text, enabling prompt injection and related
manipulation strategies [29], [30]. Additionally, token-level
perturbations can exploit vulnerabilities in attention mecha-
nisms, altering how models allocate attention weights across
input tokens and thereby influencing the final reasoning out-
come [31].
Beyond static attacks, the literature increasingly empha-
sizes adaptive adversarial strategies that evolve in response
to deployed defenses. These adaptive attacks may iteratively
probe model responses, refine adversarial prompts, and ex-
ploit feedback signals to improve attack success rates over
time. Such dynamics create a continuous attack–defense cy-
cle, in which attackers refine strategies while defenders intro-
duce hybrid detection pipelines, adversarial training methods,
and multi-layer security mechanisms [32], [33]. The resulting
cat-and-mouse dynamic highlights the importance of eval-
uating adversarial robustness under realistic adaptive threat
models rather than static perturbation scenarios.
A recent survey by Nasution et al. [34] further system-
atizes this emerging threat landscape by reviewing adversarial
prompt generation strategies ranging from direct input manip-
ulation to semantic and structural distortions, together with
benchmark datasets, robustness metrics, and preprocessing-,
model-, postprocessing-, and hybrid-defense strategies. The
survey emphasizes that adversarial evaluation must account
for evolving attack strategies rather than treating robustness
as a static property of an LLM. This perspective further
motivates adaptive evaluation settings in which adversarial
inputs can be reformulated in response to model behavior.
Complementary work has examined the defensive side of
this problem by detecting adversarial behavior directly from
LLM-generated responses. Nasution et al. [35] compared lex-
ical, contrastive, and contextual representations for response-
only adversarial classification and showed that adversarial
characteristics can be detected even when the originating
prompt is unavailable, although performance decreases sub-
stantially under cross-model evaluation. Their results also
show that lexical and contextual representations capture dif-
ferent aspects of adversarial behavior and that generalization
to unseen LLMs remains challenging. Although response-
only adversarial detection differs from the input-side evasion
setting considered here, these findings underscore the broader
difficulty of identifying adversarial behavior that transfers
across heterogeneous LLMs.
Taken together, these findings suggest that adversarial ro-
bustness in instruction-following LLM classifiers must be
studied not only at the token level but also at the discourse
and reasoning levels. LLM-based rewriting attacks therefore
represent a particularly relevant threat model for modern
classification pipelines, as they exploit the same contextual
reasoning capabilities that enable LLMs to perform complex
language understanding tasks.
E. BLACK-BOX, LABEL-ONL Y THREAT MODELS
Given that most LLM deployments are accessed via APIs,
adversarial evaluation must be framed under realistic black-
box constraints. In practical deployments, spam classifiers are
typically accessed via APIs with no exposure to logits, gra-
dients, or intermediate representations. This motivates black-
box, label-only adversarial evaluation [36].
Under this constraint, attackers must rely on output feed-
back to iteratively refine inputs. Such adaptive attacks align
with realistic adversarial scenarios where spammers itera-
tively test messages against filtering systems. Importantly,
minimal-query adaptive attacks are more reflective of oper-
ational constraints than unlimited iterative perturbations.
Recent surveys emphasize the importance of realistic threat
modeling in adversarial NLP benchmarking [37]. Evaluations
that rely solely on legacy white-box assumptions may under-
estimate practical vulnerabilities in LLM-based systems.

F. POSITIONING OF THIS WORK
Existing adversarial NLP literature has primarily evaluated
classical neural classifiers using token-level or sentence-level
perturbations. Meanwhile, LLM security research has fo-
cused on prompt injection and jailbreak attacks rather than
classification evasion.
To our knowledge, systematic comparisons between clas-
sical GAN-based rewriting and adaptive LLM-based adver-
sarial attackers on modern zero-shot LLM spam classifiers
remain limited. Existing studies often evaluate adversarial
robustness using legacy attack paradigms developed for con-
ventional neural text classifiers, which may not reflect the
behavior of instruction-following LLM classifiers. This work
addresses this gap by comparing classical GAN-based rewrit-
ing with an adaptive LLM-based attacker under a unified
black-box evaluation protocol.
This work bridges adversarial NLP and LLM security eval-
uation by benchmarking nine modern LLM spam classifiers
under a unified prompt, systematically comparing classical
GAN-based rewriting with a minimal adaptive LLM attacker,
and jointly reporting attack effectiveness under a realistic
black-box, label-only threat model.
Collectively, this background motivates the need for LLM-
native adversarial benchmarking methodologies that reflect
modern deployment realities.
III. THREAT MODEL
We formalize a realistic adversarial setting tailored to API-
deployed, prompt-based large language model (LLM) spam
classifiers. Unlike classical adversarial NLP scenarios that
assume white-box access, gradient availability, or probability
scores, our threat model reflects the operational constraints of
modern LLM-based filtering systems deployed through cloud
APIs.
The primary objective of this threat modeling exercise is
to ensure that robustness evaluation aligns with realistic ad-
versarial capabilities rather than idealized laboratory assump-
tions. In practical spam ecosystems, attackers do not have ac-
cess to model weights or internal confidence scores. Instead,
they observe binary accept/reject behavior and iteratively
refine content accordingly. Our formulation is intentionally
grounded in this operational reality.

The victim classifier satisfies several constraints that define

tations; and (iv) label-only feedback returning only SPAM
or NOT_SPAM. These constraints reflect realistic API-based
deployments and prevent reliance on gradient or score-based
adversarial optimization.

E. ADVERSARY KNOWLEDGE AND CAPABILITIES
We model a partially informed adversary consistent with
real-world spammers. The attacker is aware that the task is
spam classification and understands the binary output label
space (SPAM vs. NOT_SPAM). However, the adversary is
model-agnostic and lacks knowledge of whether the victim
is proprietary or open-source, its architecture or size, and
the composition of its training data, preventing overfitting
to specific internal mechanisms. Furthermore, manipulation
is restricted to rewriting the email text only; system-level
prompts, classifier instructions, and metadata such as sender
IP or domain remain unchanged. This design isolates input-
level semantic rewriting and excludes prompt injection or
jailbreak-style exploitation.
F. QUERY BUDGET CONSTRAINTS
Query constraints are essential for realistic benchmarking.
The classical GAN-based rewriting attack is applied using
its standard generative mechanism over the full true-positive
spam pool. In contrast, the adaptive LLM attacker is restricted
to at most two rewrite attempts: an initial rewrite followed by
a single refinement if necessary. No further iterations are per-
mitted. This bounded protocol limits the number of rewriting
attempts and enables controlled comparison between attack
strategies. The two-step design reflects a realistic black-box
interaction in which the attacker receives only the classifier
label as feedback and performs a limited refinement of the
rewritten email.

H. PRACTICAL INTERPRETATION
This threat model captures a realistic spammer workflow
in which rejected emails are iteratively rewritten to neutral-
ize persuasive cues and resubmitted under bounded query
constraints. In the LLM era, generative rewriting tools au-
tomate this adaptation loop at scale. Therefore, evaluating
only classical perturbation-based attacks underestimates real-
world adversarial risk. By formalizing a black-box, label-
only, rewrite-constrained adversary with bounded queries,
our threat model reflects contemporary deployment realities
and provides a principled foundation for comparing classical
GAN-based rewriting and adaptive LLM-based adversarial
strategies.
IV. METHODOLOGY
This section describes the experimental pipeline step-by-step
to ensure clarity and reproducibility.
A. DATASET CONSTRUCTION
The dataset is obtained from the publicly released corpus
introduced by Al-Subaiey et al. [38], which accompanies their
peer-reviewed study on a novel interpretable and robust AI
platform for phishing email detection. Rather than using an
arbitrary collection of online samples, we adopt this officially
curated dataset, which was constructed to support large-scale,
real-world phishing detection research and reported a state-
of-the-art F1 score of 0.99 in the original study. To ensure
fair evaluation across models, we construct a balanced sub-
set consisting of 1,500 spam and 1,500 non-spam emails.
This controlled class distribution prevents performance infla-
tion due to majority-class dominance and enables consistent
cross-model robustness comparison.
B. VICTIM MODEL SELECTION AND ACCESS
We evaluate a diverse set of instruction-following large lan-
guage models (LLMs) as victim classifiers. The selection
spans proprietary API-deployed systems and open-weight
models executed locally via Ollama, enabling comparison
across heterogeneous deployment settings and parameter
scales.

H. SEMANTIC PRESERVATION EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Adversarial rewriting attacks aim to alter classifier predic-
tions while preserving the underlying semantic content of
the original message. To ensure that attack success reflects
decision-boundary manipulation rather than trivial content
deletion or semantic drift, we conduct a systematic semantic
preservation evaluation between each original spam email and
its corresponding adversarial rewrites.
1) Motivation
While rewriting-based attacks constrain modifications to
stylistic framing, tone, and discourse structure, it remains
necessary to verify empirically that successful label flips are
not primarily caused by degradation of core communicative
intent. In particular, an adversarial example should retain
named entities, numerical values, offers, requests, and overall
semantic objective, even if persuasive cues such as urgency
or capitalization are softened. Without explicit semantic val-
idation, attack success could be misinterpreted as content
removal rather than robustness failure.
2) Similarity Metrics
We evaluate semantic preservation using a combination of
embedding-based and lexical similarity metrics.

of the original email and each rewritten version using a
pretrained Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model. Letxdenote

3) Evaluation Scope
Similarity is computed for both the first rewrite and the sec-
ond rewrite (when applicable) for each victim model. Because
the size of the true-positive spam pool varies across models,
similarity statistics are reported per defender model and per
attack step.
4) Outcome-Based Separation
To examine whether attack success is associated with se-
mantic degradation, we separate adversarial samples into two
groups:
•Successful flips:S={x ′|F(x ′) =NOT_SPAM},
•Failed attacks:F={x ′|F(x ′) =SPAM}.
For each group, we compute mean similarity and standard
deviation. We then evaluate whether differences in SBERT
similarity between successful and failed attacks are statisti-
cally significant using independent two-sample t-tests.
This separation enables analysis of whether classification
flips occur primarily when semantic similarity drops substan-
tially, or whether decision-boundary sensitivity is driven by
discourse-level reframing while preserving core meaning.
5) Interpretation Criteria
We interpret SBERT similarity values above 0.80 as indicat-
ing strong semantic preservation, values between 0.75 and
0.80 as moderate preservation, and values below 0.75 as
potential semantic drift. These thresholds are used descrip-
tively to identify outliers and do not directly influence attack
success measurement.
By incorporating embedding-based similarity, lexical over-
lap analysis, and outcome-based separation, this protocol en-
sures that adversarial evaluation reflects genuine robustness
assessment rather than trivial content modification.

B. ROBUSTNESS TO CLASSICAL GAN AND ADAPTIVE

Table 3 compares the effectiveness of a classical GAN-based

and 12%, respectively. These results suggest that instruction-
following adversarial rewriting is significantly more effec-
tive at bypassing LLM-based spam classifiers than classical
GAN-based approaches.
A notable pattern emerges across model scales. Smaller
models such as DeepSeek-R1:1.5B, Gemma-3:1B, and

ing ASR values of 95.78%, 42.93%, and 53.78%, respec-
tively. This indicates that robustness varies substantially
across model sizes and architectures, with larger instruction-
following models generally providing stronger resistance to
adversarial rewriting.
Overall, the comparison demonstrates that classical GAN-
based attacks substantially underestimate the evasion risk
faced by modern LLM-based spam classifiers. Adaptive
LLM-based rewriting, which operates at the discourse level
and aligns with the instruction-following paradigm of the
victim models, produces markedly higher evasion rates and
therefore provides a more realistic evaluation of classifier
robustness.
C. STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE ANAL YSIS
To assess whether the performance differences between clas-
sical GAN-based rewriting and the proposed adaptive LLM-
based attack are statistically significant, we conducted a two-
proportion z-test comparing the attack success rates (ASR) of
the two approaches.
Additionally, we estimated 95% confidence intervals for
the LLM attack success rates using bootstrap resampling. The
bootstrap procedure repeatedly sampled the attack outcomes
from the true-positive spam pool to estimate the stability of
the ASR estimates.
Table 4 presents the statistical comparison between GAN-
based and LLM-based adversarial rewriting across all vic-
tim models. The results show that the adaptive LLM attack
consistently achieves significantly higher success rates than
the classical GAN approach (p < 0.01 for all models). These
findings confirm that the observed improvements are not due
to random variation but reflect a systematic advantage of
adaptive LLM-based rewriting in black-box spam evasion.
To further illustrate the magnitude and statistical reliability
of the difference between the two attack strategies, Figure 1
compares the attack success rates of classical GAN-based
rewriting and adaptive LLM-based rewriting across all victim
models. Error bars denote 95% binomial confidence intervals
of the attack success rate (ASR) for the adaptive LLM attack.
The figure shows that the adaptive LLM strategy consistently
achieves substantially higher success rates than the classical
GAN baseline across all evaluated models. Moreover, the
confidence intervals remain well separated from the GAN
results, indicating that the performance gap is statistically
robust rather than attributable to sampling variation. This pat-
tern suggests that modern instruction-following LLMs exhibit
a discourse-level vulnerability that is not effectively exploited
by classical GAN-based rewriting methods.

To further quantify semantic degradation in adversarial
rewrites, we compute theSemantic Collapse Rate(SCR), de-

semantically degraded messages. In contrast, adaptive LLM-

that the adaptive LLM-based attack significantly outperforms

models. These results provide statistical evidence that the
improved evasion capability of instruction-following LLM
attacks represents a systematic and reproducible advantage
rather than an artifact of the dataset.
The content retention analysis further indicates that classi-
cal GAN-based attacks often succeed by generating severely
truncated or content-degraded messages, whereas LLM-

A. WHY LEGACY GAN-BASED ATTACKS UNDERESTIMATE
LLM-ERA VULNERABILITY
The comparison between GAN-based and adaptive LLM-
based attacks shows a consistent gap in adversarial effective-
ness across most victim models. Classical GAN-based rewrit-
ing achieves very low attack success rates for most larger
instruction-following LLMs, whereas adaptive LLM-based
rewriting yields substantially higher evasion rates. This con-
trast suggests that legacy sentence-level generation attacks
do not sufficiently target the discourse-level and pragmatic
cues that modern prompt-based classifiers rely on. In other
words, when the decision boundary depends not only on
lexical triggers but also on tone, framing, and communicative
style, an instruction-following adversarial rewriter is better
aligned with the victim model’s reasoning process than a
legacy GAN-based generator.
B. ROBUSTNESS IS STRONGL Y SCALE-DEPENDENT
A clear robustness gradient emerges across model scales.
Larger models such as GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3.1, Qwen-
2.5:32B, and Llama-4-Maverick:17B remain relatively resis-
tant to both attack types, although they still exhibit non-trivial
vulnerability under adaptive LLM-based rewriting. In con-
trast, smaller models such as DeepSeek-R1:1.5B, Gemma-
3:1B, and Qwen-2.5:0.5B show substantially higher attack
success rates, especially under the two-step adaptive at-
tack. This pattern suggests that scale is associated not only
with better baseline classification performance, but also with
stronger robustness to paraphrastic and discourse-level adver-
sarial manipulation. The finding is particularly relevant for
practical deployment, where lightweight models may be at-
tractive for efficiency reasons but considerably weaker under
adversarial stress.
C. ITERATIVE REWRITING MATTERS
The step-wise analysis further shows that adversarial effec-
tiveness is not solely determined by a single rewrite attempt.
The first rewriting step accounts for the majority of successful
evasions in most models, but the second step consistently
provides additional gains, especially for smaller victims. This
indicates that even minimal feedback in a black-box, label-
only setting can be exploited effectively. From a benchmark-
ing perspective, this result is important because a single-step
attack would underestimate the vulnerability of some models,
particularly those whose decision boundaries can be crossed
only after iterative reduction of spam-associated cues.
D. SEMANTIC PRESERVATION VERSUS SURFACE
TRANSFORMATION
The similarity analysis provides an important interpretive
layer for the attack results. SBERT similarity remains rel-
atively high across successful and unsuccessful adversarial
samples, indicating that the semantic content of the origi-
nal spam message is largely preserved. In contrast, BLEU,
ROUGE-L, and Jaccard show more substantial reductions,
particularly in Step 2. This divergence suggests that the
adaptive attacker succeeds primarily by altering lexical and
structural realization rather than by deleting or replacing the
underlying communicative intent. The conditional similarity
results strengthen this interpretation. Across most models,
the lexical metrics in Tables 10–12 exhibit negative∆val-
ues, indicating that successful attacks usually produce lower
lexical overlap than failed attacks, particularly during the
second rewrite step. In contrast, SBERT differences in Table 9
remain relatively small, suggesting that the semantic intent
of the original spam message is largely preserved even when
lexical and structural realization changes enough to flip the
classifier decision. In practical terms, the classifier is fooled
not because the message ceases to be spam in meaning, but
because its discourse-level realization becomes less overtly
spam-like.
E. IMPLICATIONS FOR ROBUSTNESS BENCHMARKING IN
NLP
These findings have broader implications beyond spam de-
tection. First, robustness evaluation for instruction-following
LLM classifiers should not rely exclusively on legacy ad-
versarial attacks inherited from pre-LLM text classification
settings. Second, semantic preservation analysis should be
treated as an essential component of adversarial evalua-
tion, particularly for rewriting-based attacks. Third, adap-
tive multi-step rewriting under black-box feedback provides
a realistic and scalable benchmark for modern LLM-based
classifiers. Taken together, the results support a shift from
token-level or sentence-template adversarial testing toward
discourse-aware, LLM-native robustness evaluation proto-
cols.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a comparative robustness evaluation
of instruction-following LLM classifiers under two adver-
sarial rewriting paradigms: classical GAN-based rewriting
and a two-step adaptive LLM-based attacker. Using nine
victim models and a balanced spam detection dataset, we

differences but more consistently negative lexical differ-
ences, indicating that effective evasion is associated more
with surface-form reformulation than with semantic drift.
This reinforces the interpretation that modern LLM-based
adversarial rewriting operates primarily at the discourse and
stylistic levels. This suggests that adversarial success is driven
primarily by discourse-level and surface-form transformation
rather than semantic deletion. Taken together, these findings
indicate that legacy adversarial attacks can underestimate
the vulnerability of prompt-based LLM classifiers, and that
adaptive LLM-based rewriting provides a more realistic ro-
bustness benchmark for modern NLP classification systems.
Our content retention analysis further reveals that success-
ful LLM-based attacks preserve substantially more semantic
content than classical GAN-based rewriting, indicating that
modern adversarial strategies exploit discourse-level vulner-
abilities in spam classifiers while maintaining the original
message meaning.
Future work should extend this evaluation to additional at-
tacker models, include human assessment of semantic preser-
vation, and investigate defense strategies that explicitly re-
duce sensitivity to surface-level discourse manipulation while
preserving classification accuracy.
